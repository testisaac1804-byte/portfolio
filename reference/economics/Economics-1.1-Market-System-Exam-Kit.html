Economics 1.1 — The Market System

1.1.1 The economic problem  →  PPF  →  Demand  →  Supply  →  Market Equilibrium  ·  Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) — taught + exam skills

🚀 Start here (2 minutes)

Your exam in one line: Edexcel 4EC1 Paper 1 = 1 hour 30 min, 80 marks, 4 compulsory questions × 20 marks. You get 6 multiple-choice questions per paper, a 9-mark "assess" in Q2 and Q3, and the last question is always a 12-mark "evaluate". That's it. Everything below is built to feed those questions.

What you must be able to do by the end

1. Say itDefine scarcity, opportunity cost, demand, supply, equilibrium price/quantity, excess demand, excess supply.
2. Draw itPPF, demand curve, supply curve, equilibrium, and both shifts — fully labelled.
3. Move itDecide correctly: is this a movement along the curve (price changed) or a shift (anything else changed)?
4. Chain itShift → new price → new quantity → who wins/loses. That chain IS the mark scheme.
5. Balance itFor 9 and 12 marks: two viewpoints + a supported judgement. One side = capped at Level 1.

60-second summary of the whole topic

Wants are unlimited, resources are finite → scarcity → we must choose → every choice has an opportunity cost. A PPF draws those choices for a whole economy (on the line = fully used, inside = unemployed resources, outside = unobtainable; the curve shifts out with growth, in with disaster).

Zoom into one market: demand is "willingness and ability to buy at a given price" (slopes down — law of demand). Supply is "willingness and ability to sell at a given price" (slopes up — higher price = higher profit motive). Where they cross = market equilibrium, giving equilibrium price Pe and quantity Qe. Above Pe = excess supply (surplus), below Pe = excess demand (shortage). Market forces then push price back to Pe on their own.

1.1.1a–b — Scarcity, choice & opportunity cost

The whole subject in 4 moves

1. Wants are infinitePeople always want more — new phone, bigger house, better holiday. Never satisfied.
2. Resources are finiteLand, labour, capital and enterprise exist in limited amounts. This is scarcity.
3. Therefore chooseThe economic problem = matching limited resources to unlimited wants and needs → choices must be made.
4. Therefore costChoosing one thing means giving up the next best alternative = opportunity cost.

Definitions the examiner wants (word-for-word level)

Scarcity — the basic economic problem that resources are limited in supply relative to the unlimited wants and needs of people.
The economic problem — how to allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants and needs.
Needs — goods/services essential for survival (food, water, shelter). Wants — goods/services that are desired but not essential.
Opportunity cost — the benefit lost from the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. (His teacher's wording: "the benefits lost of the next best alternative — the option you did not choose.")

The #1 lost mark on opportunity cost: students write "what you gave up". The mark needs next best — you only give up ONE thing (the next best alternative), not all the things you didn't buy. And it is the benefit lost, not the item.

The 4 factors of production (all four are always scarce)

FactorWhat it isReward (payment)Island School example

The 3 fundamental economic questions

What to produce?How to allocate scarce resources between competing uses — tanks or hospitals? Which goods/services?
How to produce?Which method — labour-intensive or capital-intensive? Efficient technology?
For whom to produce?How is output distributed — who gets to consume it? By price, need, or government decision?

Exam sentence frame (works for almost any "opportunity cost" question): "The opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone. By choosing X, the [agent] gives up the benefit of Y, which is the next most preferred option."

1.1.1c–e — The Production Possibility Curve (PPF)

Definition: A PPF shows the different combinations of two goods that can be produced if all factors of production (resources) are fully and efficiently employed.
  • Assumption 1: only two goods/categories are produced (e.g. consumer goods vs capital goods).
  • Assumption 2: all resources are fully and efficiently used.

Consumer goods = purchased by households (B2C), e.g. food, phones. Capital goods = purchased by firms to produce other goods (B2B), e.g. machinery, robots.

What the PPF must show (spec 1.1.1d — memorise these 5)

#What it showsWhere / how
1The maximum productive potential of an economyAny point ON the curve (A, B, C, D) — the frontier itself
2Fully employed vs unemployed resourcesON the curve = fully employed; INSIDE (F) = unemployed/inefficient (recession, unused labour)
3Opportunity costMoving ALONG the curve: give up some consumer goods to get more capital goods — that lost output is the opportunity cost
4Positive / negative economic growthCurve shifting OUTWARD (growth) or INWARD (negative growth)
5Possible vs unobtainable productionOn or inside = possible; OUTSIDE (X) = unobtainable with current resources

Worked example (from your teacher's deck)

Point B = 14m consumer + 4m capital. Point C = 8m consumer + 7m capital.
Moving B → C: gain 3m capital goods, lose 6m consumer goods. Opportunity cost = 6 million consumer goods.

Write it that way in the exam: "the opportunity cost of the extra 3m capital goods is the 6m consumer goods forgone."

Shifts of the PPF

OUTWARD shift = economic growth ↑ Causes: new technology/innovation · improved efficiency (higher productivity) · better education & training (better labour quality) · increase in the quantity of capital or labour · discovery/extraction of new natural resources (e.g. oil).
INWARD shift = negative economic growth ↓ Causes: natural disasters (drought, tsunami, earthquake, floods) · war/civil conflict destroying factors of production · large-scale labour migration/emigration · natural resources running out.

Movement along vs shift of the PPF — do not mix these up

SituationYes/noWhat happens
War breaks out✗INWARD shift — factors of production destroyed
A very dry summer✗INWARD (or slower growth) — land/agriculture hit
Improved education✗OUTWARD — better quality labour
Producing more capital goods—MOVEMENT along the curve now, but OUTWARD shift in future (more investment → growth)
Discovery of new technology✗OUTWARD shift
An economy operating above potential✗Impossible — you cannot be outside the frontier; it only runs at max (on the curve) or below it
Top-mark insight: producing more capital goods (point B rather than A) sacrifices consumption now but gives greater growth in the future — more capital = more investment = better quantity and quality of factors of production. That one sentence is why B wins the "which leads to greater growth" question.

1.1.3a–c — Demand

Definition (2-mark, two parts!): Demand is the willingness (1) and ability (2) of consumers to buy a good or service at a given price in a given time period.
"Willingness and ability" — both words must appear. Being willing to buy a Ferrari without the money is not demand. That's why it's called effective demand.

The law of demand

As the price of a product falls, quantity demanded rises. As the price rises, quantity demanded falls. There is an inverse (negative) relationship between price and quantity demanded → so the demand curve slopes downwards, left to right.

The two things that get confused (this is where marks die)

 MOVEMENT along the demand curveSHIFT of the demand curve
Change in…Change in quantity demandedChange in demand
Caused byONLY a change in the price of the good itselfAny non-price determinant of demand
What the diagram showsSame curve, slide from P₁ to P₂A NEW curve D₁ → D₂
Right/left rule—New curve on the RIGHT = increase; LEFT = decrease
Paper 1 killer: never write "the price rose so demand shifted to the left". A price rise causes a contraction of demand — a movement up along the same curve. Marks are lost for this in MCQs and in 6/9/12-mark answers.

Substitute vs complement in one line: substitutes compete (Coke/Pepsi, Uber/MTR) — one's price ↑ makes the other's demand ↑. Complements are consumed together (cars/petrol, phones/cases) — one's price ↑ makes the other's demand ↓.

1.1.3d–f — Supply

Definition (2 marks, two parts): Supply is the willingness and ability of producers to sell a good or service at a given price in a given time period.

The law of supply

As price rises, quantity supplied rises. As price falls, quantity supplied falls. Positive/direct relationship → the supply curve slopes upwards, left to right. Reason: a higher price = higher profit per unit = an incentive for firms to produce more (and it makes production of the extra units worthwhile).

1.1.3g–j — Market equilibrium, excess demand & excess supply

Market equilibrium — the price at which quantity demanded = quantity supplied. The market is "cleared" — no shortage and no surplus.
Equilibrium price (Pe) — the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
Equilibrium quantity (Qe) — the quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price.
Also called the market clearing price — because at this price the market clears: every willing buyer finds a seller and vice versa, leaving no excess.

Excess supply vs excess demand

Price ABOVE Pe → EXCESS SUPPLY (surplus) Qs > Qd. Firms are left with unsold stock. Rivals cut prices to clear stock → price falls back to Pe.
Price BELOW Pe → EXCESS DEMAND (shortage) Qd > Qs. Consumers can't get the good (queues, rationing, black markets). Firms raise prices — buyers still willing → price rises back to Pe.
How to read the gap correctly: the size of the excess is always the horizontal distance between the two curves at that price. Read across from the given price to the S curve and to the D curve, then subtract. Always say which is bigger.

Sentences: "When price increases, quantity supplied increases." "When price increases, quantity demanded decreases."
"The equilibrium price is also known as the market clearing price" — because at this price the market clears: all goods supplied are bought, so there is no surplus and no shortage left over.

Calculating excess demand / supply (2-mark "calculate" questions)

Excess demand = Qd − Qs   |   Excess supply = Qs − Qd

  • Qd 4,000 chairs, Qs 7,000 → excess SUPPLY of 3,000 (7,000 − 4,000).
  • Qd 10,000 mugs, Qs 3,000 → excess DEMAND of 7,000 (10,000 − 3,000).
  • Qd 12,000 lemons, Qs 6,650 → excess DEMAND of 5,350 (12,000 − 6,650).
  • Over several weeks: excess 2,000 per week × 4 weeks = 8,000 units accumulated. Always multiply when the question says "over a four-week period".

How shifts change the equilibrium (spec 1.1.3h)

ChangeCurvePeQeMovement along the other curve

Demand ↓D shifts LEFT↓↓Movement down along S

Model chain — face masks, Hong Kong, Covid-19 (D shifts right)

"The pandemic is a change in special circumstances, a non-price determinant of demand. Consumers want more masks at every price, so demand shifts from D₁ to D₂. The equilibrium moves from A to B: equilibrium price rises from P₁ to P₂ and equilibrium quantity rises from Q₁ to Q₂. The rise in price causes a movement along (extension of) the supply curve — producers are incentivised by the higher price to supply more."

Model chain — sales tax on tobacco (S shifts left)

"The tax is a non-price determinant of supply: it raises producers' costs. Supply shifts from S₁ to S₂. Equilibrium moves from A to B: price rises from P₁ to P₂ and quantity falls from Q₁ to Q₂. The higher price causes a contraction of demand (movement up along the demand curve)."

The universal market sentence (learn this shape, swap the words):
"[Factor] is a non-price determinant of [demand/supply]. This causes [demand/supply] to shift [right/left]. Equilibrium price [rises/falls] and equilibrium quantity [rises/falls]. The price change causes a movement [along] the [other] curve."

📊 Diagram lab — what earns the 3 "draw" marks

Edexcel's own definition: a Draw question is 3 marks = 3 separate awardable points. On a supply-and-demand diagram the examiner is looking for awardable labels and direction, so every diagram you draw in the exam should carry all five of these:
  1. Axes labelled — "Price" (y) and "Quantity" (x).
  2. Each curve labelled — D, S, or D₁/D₂, S₁/S₂.
  3. Key points marked and labelled — P₁, Q₁, P₂, Q₂ and/or the equilibrium point E (or A→B).
  4. Arrows in the right direction — an arrow on the shifted curve (→ right or ← left) and, for market answers, a small arrow showing price ↑ or ↓.
  5. Shifted curves dashed + numbered (D₁→D₂) so the change is visible.

Your 6 must-draw diagrams

#DiagramMust show
1PPFBoth axes labelled (capital / consumer goods), curved line, points on the curve (full employment), a point inside (unemployed), a point outside (unobtainable), outward shift arrow for growth
2Demand curveDownward-sloping D, axes labelled, movement from P₁Q₁ → P₂Q₂
3Shift in demandD₁ and dashed D₂ to the right (increase) or left (decrease), arrow showing direction
4Supply curve / shiftUpward-sloping S; dashed S₂ with direction arrow
5D and S crossing at E, dashed lines to Pe and Qe, both axes labelled
6Excess demand / excess supplyA price line above (surplus) or below (shortage) Pe, and the horizontal gap between S and D bracketed and labelled
The 3 most common diagram errors (each costs marks):
  1. Unlabelled axes. No "Price"/"Quantity" = you've thrown away an awardable point instantly.
  2. Drawing a shift when the question is a movement. If the price changed, the curve does NOT move.
  3. No P/Q labels or arrows after a shift — the examiner can't see that you know price changed.
Speed tip: in the exam, draw the diagram FIRST (2 minutes), then write the explanation referencing it. Your prose then becomes "as shown in the diagram…" and you rarely drop diagram marks.

Model answer — "What is meant by opportunity cost?" (2 marks)

Answer: "The benefit lost from the next best alternative forgone (1) when a choice is made about how to use scarce resources (2)."
Why full marks: two separate awardable parts; says "next best" and "benefit lost"; links to scarcity/choice.

Model answer — "Explain one reason why the demand curve for X may shift to the left." (3 marks)

Answer: "A fall in consumers' income (1) means consumers have less money to spend (2) so they will buy less of the product at every price, shifting demand to the left (3)."
Why 3 marks: statement of fact (a reason) + two expansion points, and it lands in the context of the question. No diagram gap since the question says "explain".

Model answer — 6-mark Analyse

Question: "With reference to the data, analyse the impact of a rise in the price of petrol on the market for large cars."

Answer (full marks): "Petrol and large cars are complementary goods — they are consumed together. If the price of petrol rises (as shown by the data rising from $X to $Y), the total cost of running a large car increases. This reduces the demand for large cars, so the demand curve shifts to the left from D₁ to D₂. As a result the equilibrium price of large cars falls from P₁ to P₂ and the equilibrium quantity falls from Q₁ to Q₂. Car dealers would see lower revenue and may respond by cutting prices further or switching to smaller models. Buyers are better off as they pay less, but producers and their workers may face reduced sales and possible job losses."

Why Level 3: correct term applied (complements), uses the data (AO3), a coherent chain shift → price → quantity, and a developed consequence for consumers/producers. No need for "on the other hand" — analyse is one-directional.

Model answer — 9-mark Assess

Question: "Assess whether an increase in the price of a good will always lead to a fall in the quantity demanded."

Answer (Level 3): "According to the law of demand, price and quantity demanded have an inverse relationship, so a rise in price should lead to a contraction of demand — a movement up along the demand curve. Higher prices also encourage consumers to switch to substitutes, which reduces demand further.

However, this assumes ceteris paribus. If demand is price inelastic because the good is a necessity, has few substitutes or takes a small share of income, a price rise may barely reduce quantity demanded. Rising price can even coincide with rising quantity demanded if demand is shifting right at the same time — for example, during a shortage consumers expect further price rises and buy more, or when incomes rise so much that even the higher price is affordable. The data shows [quote figures].

On balance, a price rise will normally reduce quantity demanded, but it is not automatic: it depends on the elasticity of demand and on what is happening to the non-price determinants at the same time."

Why Level 3: both viewpoints, balanced, evidenced with the data, and a supported judgement that answers the "always" in the question. The word "always" is the cue to disagree in part.

Model answer — 12-mark Evaluate

Question: "With reference to the information given and your knowledge of economics, evaluate the impact of the government imposing a maximum price on a good such as rented housing."

Answer (Level 3): "A maximum price is a legal limit on how much firms can charge. If it is set below the equilibrium price, the market is artificially cheap: quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, creating excess demand (a shortage). In the rented housing market this means more people want to rent but fewer landlords are willing to supply at the lower rent. Landlords' revenue per property falls, so they may withdraw properties from the market or reduce maintenance, making housing quality worse. Queues, waiting lists and possible black markets may develop, and allocation is no longer by price but by luck or favour.

On the other hand, the policy has clear benefits. Existing tenants pay lower rent, which raises their real disposable income and reduces poverty and housing stress — a strong argument in a city like Hong Kong where housing is a large share of spending. It may also reduce labour mobility problems if workers can afford to live near work.

On balance, whether the maximum price works depends on how far below equilibrium it is set and how elastic supply is. A small gap creates a small shortage; a large gap creates severe shortages and long-term under-investment in housing. The policy appears to benefit consumers in the short run but risks damaging supply and quality in the long run, so it is likely to be only a partial solution and would work better alongside policies to increase the supply of housing."

Why Level 3: defines the concept, develops both sides with real consequences, quotes the data context, gives a conditional judgement ("depends on…") and a clear final conclusion. Note the structure: define → one side → other side (explicitly signalled) → conditional judgement → conclusion.

    A An increasing number of drivers buy electric cars  B Demand for new cars has increased  C New technologies reduce the time needed to extract oil  D There is an increase in world oil supplies
    A. Electric cars are substitutes for petrol-driven cars, so more EVs = fewer petrol buyers = demand for petrol shifts left. B would increase demand for petrol (complements). C and D affect supply of oil, not demand for petrol.
  6. What is likely to have caused a supply curve for agricultural output to shift to the left?
    A Subsidies provided to farmers  B Favourable weather conditions  C Higher cost of fertilisers  D Greater number of farmers
    C. Higher fertiliser cost raises production costs → supply shifts left. A, B and D all increase supply (shift right).
  7. The supply curve for smartphones shifts to the right. What is likely to have caused it?
    A A fall in the price of laptops  B A lower sales tax on electronic goods  C A reduction in income tax rates  D A fall in the price of smartphones
    B. A lower sales tax cuts producers' costs → supply shifts right. A and C affect demand. D is the trap — a fall in the price of smartphones causes a movement along the supply curve, not a shift.

B. 2-mark & 3-mark

  1. (2) What is meant by "excess supply"?
    "The situation where the quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded (1) at a price above the equilibrium price (2)." Two separate parts — the gap, and when it happens.
  2. (3) Explain one reason why the demand curve for face masks shifts to the right.
    "A pandemic is a change in special circumstances — a non-price determinant of demand (1). Consumers become more worried about infection and want more masks at every price (2), so the demand curve shifts to the right (3)."
  3. (2) Calculate: quantity demanded of lemons is 12,000 and quantity supplied is 6,650. What is the market situation?
    Excess demand (1) of 5,350 lemons (2): 12,000 − 6,650 = 5,350. Method mark then answer mark — always write the subtraction.
  4. (3) Draw a diagram to show the market for tobacco after the government imposes a sales tax.
    Your diagram needs: labelled Price/Quantity axes (1); S₁ and a dashed S₂ shifted to the LEFT with an arrow (2); new equilibrium marked showing P↑ from P₁→P₂ and Q↓ from Q₁→Q₂ (3).

C. Extended answers

  1. (6) Analyse the effect of a drought on the market for wheat and on farmers' revenue.
    A drought is a change in the non-price determinant of supply, natural factors (1). It destroys crops and raises farmers' costs, so supply shifts to the left from S₁ to S₂ (2). Equilibrium price rises from P₁ to P₂ and equilibrium quantity falls from Q₁ to Q₂ (3). The higher price causes a contraction of demand — consumers who can't afford the higher price buy less (4). Farmers' revenue may actually rise, because wheat is a necessity with inelastic demand: the percentage fall in quantity is smaller than the percentage rise in price (5). However, farmers whose own harvest failed cannot benefit — they have nothing to sell, and may face bankruptcy (6).
  2. (9) Assess whether a rise in the price of a good will always reduce the quantity demanded.
    Structure for full marks (Level 3):
    Side 1 — yes: law of demand = inverse relationship; a price rise causes a contraction of demand (movement up the curve); consumers switch to substitutes. Quote the data.
    Side 2 — not always: if demand is price inelastic (necessity, few substitutes, small % of income), the fall in quantity may be tiny; if demand is simultaneously shifting right (higher incomes, a fad, expectations of further rises), quantity demanded can rise with price; a good can also be a status/Veblen good where a higher price signals quality.
    Judgement: "On balance a price rise normally reduces quantity demanded, but it is not automatic — it depends on elasticity and on what else is happening to the non-price determinants. The word 'always' makes the statement too strong."
  3. (12) With reference to the information given and your knowledge of economics, evaluate the impact on the market for rented housing of the government setting a maximum rent below the equilibrium price.
    Use the 12-mark model answer in the Exam Skills tab — same structure: define the maximum price → excess demand/shortage → effects on landlords (withdraw properties, poor maintenance) and on allocation (queues, black markets) → then the benefits (lower rent, higher real disposable income, less poverty) → conditional judgement (depends how far below equilibrium, and how elastic supply is) → supported conclusion ("only a partial solution; needs supply-side policies too").

D. Definitions self-test (say each out loud, then check)

Show the 12 definitions you must know word-perfect
  1. Scarcity — resources are limited in supply relative to unlimited wants and needs.
  2. The economic problem — how to allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants and needs.
  3. Opportunity cost — the benefit lost from the next best alternative forgone.
  4. PPF — the different combinations of two goods that can be produced if all resources are fully and efficiently employed.
  5. Demand — the willingness and ability of consumers to buy a good at a given price in a given time period.
  6. Law of demand — as price falls, quantity demanded rises (inverse relationship).
  7. Supply — the willingness and ability of producers to sell a good at a given price in a given time period.
  8. Law of supply — as price rises, quantity supplied rises (direct relationship).
  9. Market equilibrium — the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied; the market is cleared of shortages and surpluses.
  10. Equilibrium price — the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied (also the market clearing price).
  11. Excess demand (shortage) — quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, at a price below equilibrium.
  12. Excess supply (surplus) — quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, at a price above equilibrium.

Plus the rewards: land = rent, labour = wages, capital = interest, enterprise = profit.
