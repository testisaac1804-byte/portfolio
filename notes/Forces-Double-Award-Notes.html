The 4 Big Ideas

1. Movement
Speed is how fast; acceleration is how fast the speed changes. Everything is measured from graphs and formulas (Lessons 1–6).
2. Forces cause acceleration
No unbalanced force → no acceleration. F = ma is the engine of the whole topic (Lessons 5–7).
3. Everything stops
Stopping distance = thinking + braking, and energy explains why speed is so dangerous (Lesson 8).
4. Springs are predictable… until they're not
Hooke's Law: stretch is proportional to force, up to the elastic limit (Lessons 9–10).

Scalars vs Vectors (objective 1.13, 1.14)

Scalar — size onlyVector — size AND direction
mass, time, distance, speed, temperature, energy, lengthforce, velocity, acceleration, displacement, weight
Memory trick: ask "which way?" — "5 kg… which way?" = nonsense → scalar. "5 N… which way?" = makes sense → vector. Force is a vector (pushing left ≠ pushing right).

Adding forces along a line → Resultant force (1.15)

  • Same direction → add: 3 N + 4 N = 7 N
  • Opposite directions → subtract, pointing the way the bigger force points: 3 N ← vs 4 N → = 1 N →
  • The single combined force = the resultant (unbalanced) force.

⚖️ The golden rule of forces

Resultant force = 0 → object stays at rest or keeps moving at constant speed.
Resultant force ≠ 0 → object accelerates in the direction of that force.
This one idea explains terminal velocity, stopping, floating, everything.

Types of force (1.12)

Non-contact forces (act through fields, no touching)
• Gravitational (between masses)
• Electrostatic (between charges)
• Magnetic (between magnets)
Contact forces (need touching)
• Friction (opposes motion)
• Air resistance / drag (friction in air)
• Tension (pull in ropes/strings)
• Normal reaction (surface pushing back)
• Thrust (engines pushing forward)

Fields: an electric field applies a non-contact force to a charged object; a gravitational field applies a non-contact force to any mass.

What forces do (1.11)

Forces cause changes of speed (accelerate/decelerate), changes of direction (turning, circular motion), or changes of shape (stretching, squashing).

Lessons 2 & 3 — Speed & Acceleration

Speed (1.4)

speed = distance ÷ time distance = speed × time  ·  time = distance ÷ speed  ·  unit: m/s (or km/h, mph, knots…)
  • Units: m/s (metres per second). km/h → m/s: ÷ 3.6. So 50 km/h ≈ 14 m/s, 100 km/h ≈ 28 m/s.
  • Average speed = total distance ÷ total time (the car rarely moves at one exact speed).
  • 'Speed' is a scalar (no direction); 'velocity' is its vector cousin.

Acceleration (1.6)

a = (v − u) ÷ t v = final velocity  ·  u = initial velocity  ·  t = time taken  ·  unit: m/s²

m/s² reads as "metres per second, per second" — how many m/s of speed are gained every single second. A car at 4 m/s² gains 4 m/s of speed each second.

  • Speeding up → positive acceleration. Slowing down → deceleration (negative acceleration).
  • Starting "from rest" always means u = 0.
  • Acceleration is a vector (it has direction).

Worked examples

✏️ Example A

A car accelerates from 12 m/s to 24 m/s in 15 s.
a = (24 − 12) ÷ 15 = 12 ÷ 15 = 0.80 m/s²

✏️ Example B

A car accelerates from rest at 2.3 m/s² for 7.1 s.
Rearrange: v = u + at → v = 0 + 2.3 × 7.1 = 16 m/s

✏️ Example C

A plane reaches take-off speed 55 m/s after accelerating at 4.8 m/s² for 9.8 s.
Rearrange: u = v − at = 55 − (4.8 × 9.8) = 55 − 47.0 = 8.0 m/s

✏️ Example D

Usain Bolt accelerates from rest to 12 m/s at 2.1 m/s².
t = (v − u) ÷ a = 12 ÷ 2.1 = 5.7 s

Ticker tape — how it works (exam-favourite practical)

  • The timer makes 50 dots per second → time between dots = 1/50 = 0.02 s.
  • Speed: measure a gap between two dots (convert cm → m), divide by 0.02 s. Examples: 1 cm gap → 0.01 ÷ 0.02 = 0.5 m/s.
  • Acceleration: find speed u at one gap and speed v at another gap 10 dots later (time between them = 10 × 0.02 = 0.2 s), then a = (v − u) ÷ 0.2.
  • Reading a tape (dots = distance per 0.02 s):
    • gaps equal → constant speed
    • gaps growing → accelerating
    • gaps shrinking → decelerating
    • gaps growing faster → accelerating more
  • Falling plasticine experiment: g ≈ 9.8 m/s². Your measured value is usually a bit lower because of air resistance and friction on the tape (that's the mark-scheme reason).

Oil-drop version (same idea, car leaking oil)

✏️ Example — car dripping oil every 1.4 s

First two drops 3.8 m apart; a later pair 4.7 s afterwards, 8.3 m apart.
v₁ = 3.8 ÷ 1.4 = 2.71 m/s  ·  v₂ = 8.3 ÷ 1.4 = 5.93 m/s
a = (5.93 − 2.71) ÷ 4.7 ≈ 0.68 m/s²

Accuracy vs Reliability (asked in every practical exam)

Accuracy — closeness to the true valueReliability — confidence in your readings
Better technique, avoiding zero errors, using more sensitive instruments, measuring carefullyRepeat readings, calculate an average, plot with a line of best fit, eliminate anomalies

Light gates + ramp experiment

  • Research question: how does ramp height affect a trolley's acceleration?
  • Light gate 1 measures the trolley's starting speed u, light gate 2 the final speed v; the computer times the 0.2 s between them → a = (v − u) ÷ t.
  • Higher ramp → bigger acceleration (more of the weight acts along the slope). Graph of height vs acceleration: straight line through the origin (directly proportional).

Lesson 4 — Motion Graphs (1.3, 1.7, 1.8, 1.9)

📏 Distance–time
Gradient = speed
Flat = stationary. Straight up = constant speed. Steeper = faster. Curving up = accelerating.
📈 Velocity–time
Gradient = acceleration
Area under = distance
Flat = constant velocity. Up = accelerating. Down = decelerating.

The area rule — where marks are won

Steady speed: 20 m/s for 10 s        Speeding up: 0 → 20 m/s over 10 s
┌────────────────┐   height 20 m/s   ╱│
│                │   width  10 s     ╱ │
│                │                   ╱  │  triangle
│                │   area = 20×10   ╱   │  area = ½ × 10 × 20
└────────────────┘   = 200 m        ╱    │        = 100 m
0                10s                ╱─────┘
  • Split a complicated graph into triangles + rectangles, add the areas.
  • Triangle: ½ × base × height · Rectangle: base × height.
  • Wavy parts: count squares, or estimate with strips — but IGCSE graphs are made of straight lines.

Worked examples

✏️ Area question

Car at constant 20 m/s for 10 s → distance = 20 × 10 = 200 m.
Car accelerating from rest to 20 m/s over 10 s → distance = ½ × 10 × 20 = 100 m.
Same top speed, but it averaged only 10 m/s — so exactly half the distance. 📉

✏️ Gradient question

Speed rises from 10 m/s to 30 m/s between t = 5 s and t = 15 s:
a = gradient = (30 − 10) ÷ (15 − 5) = 20 ÷ 10 = 2.0 m/s²

Bouncing ball (extension, but worth understanding)

  • Distance–time: a series of curved humps (each bounce, the ball slows going up, speeds up coming down), each hump shorter than the last because the ball loses energy.
  • Velocity–time: zigzag — straight lines sloping down while rising (decelerating at g), flipping sign at the top, sloping up while falling back (accelerating at g), then an instant bounce where velocity reverses and shrinks a bit each time.
⚠️ Traps: distance ≠ displacement on return trips (distance adds up, displacement cancels out). On a v–t graph, acceleration comes from gradient and distance from area — swapping these is the #1 graph mistake.

Lessons 5 & 6 — Forces, F = ma & Newton's Laws (1.17)

Newton's Three Laws

LawStatementIn plain words
IObjects stay at rest or moving at uniform velocity unless acted on by a resultant force.No net force → nothing changes. Moving things keep moving.
IIAcceleration ∝ resultant force.F = ma — the formula behind everything.
IIIEvery force has an equal and opposite force on the other object.Push a wall, the wall pushes you back equally.
F = m × a F = resultant (unbalanced) force / N  ·  m = mass / kg  ·  a = acceleration / m/s² Rearranged: m = F ÷ a  ·  a = F ÷ m

1 newton = the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s².

⚠️ F is always the UNBALANCED (resultant) force. If forces balance, F = 0 and a = 0 — no acceleration even if forces are huge.

Worked examples

✏️ F = ma

30 N acts on a 10 kg object → a = 30 ÷ 10 = 3 m/s².
Car 1200 kg braking force 7600 N → a = 7600 ÷ 1200 = 6.33 m/s² deceleration.

✏️ Friction block

A 2.7 kg block slides at 2.3 m/s; friction is 2.6 N (opposes motion).
Deceleration = F ÷ m = 2.6 ÷ 2.7 = 0.96 m/s². Distance to stop: v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 2.3² − 2(0.96)s → s = 5.29 ÷ 1.93 ≈ 2.7 m.

The trolley experiment (Lesson 5)

  • Research question: relationship between resultant force and acceleration of a trolley.
  • Independent variable (what you change, x-axis): resultant force (0.5 N → 3.5 N via weights)
  • Dependent variable (what you measure, y-axis): acceleration of the trolley (light gates)
  • Control variables (keep fixed): angle of slope (horizontal), mass (1 kg), starting point, medium (air)
  • Repeat 3×, take the mean, plot mean acceleration vs force.
  • Result: straight line through the origin → force and acceleration are directly proportional → doubling force doubles acceleration.
  • Mass of trolley = force ÷ acceleration = the gradient⁻¹ of the graph (a = F/m, so F/a = m). Typical result ≈ 2.3 kg.

Variables & fair tests (applies to every practical)

  • Independent (input): the one thing you change.
  • Dependent (outcome): the thing that responds, which you measure.
  • Control: everything else, kept the same so the test is fair.

Circular motion — the sneak question

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating, because its direction is constantly changing — and velocity is a vector. Direction change = velocity change = acceleration. 🎯

Astronaut example: in space she can be weightless, but her mass stays the same.

W = m × g g = gravitational field strength ≈ 10 N/kg on Earth (accepted value 9.8 N/kg)

✏️ Example

Jeremy has mass 74 kg → W = 74 × 10 = 740 N.

The falling object story — 5 stages

StageForcesWhat happens
1. Just droppedOnly weight (down)Unbalanced → accelerates at ~10 m/s²
2. A few seconds laterWeight down, some air resistance upResultant force smaller → still accelerating, but less
3. ~20 s laterAir resistance = weight (balanced)Resultant = 0 → acceleration = 0 → TERMINAL VELOCITY
4. Parachute opensAir resistance shoots up (big surface area)Air resistance > weight → unbalanced force upwards → decelerates
5. LaterAir resistance decreases as speed dropsBalanced again → new, slower terminal velocity → gentle landing

Why terminal velocity happens: as speed increases, air resistance increases. Eventually air resistance equals weight, the resultant force becomes zero, and the object falls at constant speed. No unbalanced force → no acceleration.

⚠️ Terminal velocity = constant speed, NOT zero speed. The skydiver is still falling!

  • Curve flattening = acceleration decreasing (air resistance growing)
  • Horizontal line = balanced forces, terminal velocity
  • Steep drop = big upward unbalanced force (deceleration)
  • Final low flat line = slow terminal velocity
🚀 On the Moon: no air → no air resistance → nothing ever balances the weight → the object keeps accelerating forever. No terminal velocity exists without air.

Lesson 8 — Stopping Distance (1.19, 1.20)

Stopping distance = Thinking distance + Braking distance

The two phases

PhaseWhat happensFormula
Thinking (reaction)Driver sees the danger, brain decides, foot moves to brake — car still moving at full speedThinking distance = speed × reaction time
BrakingBrakes create friction (opposes motion); kinetic energy is converted to heat; car slows to stopBraking distance = mv² ÷ 2F (from ½mv² = F × d)

Worked examples

✏️ Thinking distance

14 m/s with reaction 0.6 s → d = 14 × 0.6 = 8.4 m.
Same speed, distracted, 1.5 s → d = 14 × 1.5 = 21 m. 😬
On the 100 km/h highway (28 m/s), 1.5 s → d = 28 × 1.5 = 42 m.

✏️ Braking distance

Family car: 900 kg, brakes 8000 N, speed 17 m/s.
½ × 900 × 17² = 8000 × d → d = 130 050 ÷ 8000 ≈ 16 m.
Add thinking (17 × 1.0 s = 17 m) → stopping ≈ 33 m.

⚠️ The most asked "explain" question in the topic

Double the speed → quadruple the braking distance.
Because KE = ½mv²: doubling v squares it (2² = 4) → 4× kinetic energy → brakes must do 4× work → 4× distance.

Mark-scheme phrase: "Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, so doubling the speed increases the kinetic energy (and so the work done by the brakes, and braking distance) by four times."

Factors affecting stopping distance — memorise the two columns

ProblemThinking distanceBraking distance
Driver tired, drunk, on drugs↑ longer reaction timeNone (brakes don't care about the driver's state)
Distracted (phone, passengers)↑ longer reaction timeNone
Wet or icy roadNone↑ less friction
Worn tyres / brake padsNone↑ less friction
Car overloaded (more mass)None↑ more KE → more distance
Speeding↑ (more distance per second)↑↑↑↑ quadrupled

The energy story

  • Moving car has kinetic energy: KE = ½mv² (J)
  • Brakes convert KE into heat (thermal) energy
  • The KE transferred = the work done by the brakes: Work = F × d (J = N × m)
  • So ½mv² = F × d → braking distance d = mv² ÷ 2F

Reaction time rider experiment

Catch a falling ruler; convert fall distance to time: d = ½at² → t = √(2d ÷ g).
13 cm fall → t = √(0.26 ÷ 10) ≈ 0.16 s.

Lessons 9 & 10 — Hooke's Law (1.22, 1.23, 1.24)

F = k × x F = force / N  ·  k = spring constant / N/m  ·  x = EXTENSION / m
  • Extension = stretched length − original length (NOT the total length — a classic trap).
  • k (spring constant) = the stiffness of the spring — how hard it fights being stretched. Big k = stiff spring. k = gradient of the F–x graph.
  • Hooke's Law: extension is directly proportional to force — double the force, double the extension — but only up to the elastic limit.
  • Past the elastic limit, the graph bends; the spring is permanently deformed (plastic) and won't snap back.

Elastic vs Plastic vs Hookean — don't mix these up

TermMeaningForce–extension graph
ElasticCan stretch and returns to original shape when force removedAny shape (straight or curved)
PlasticCan stretch and keeps the new shape (permanently deformed)—
HookeanObeys Hooke's LawStraight line through the origin (up to elastic limit)
  • Springs and metal wires: Hookean (straight line through origin, then bend at elastic limit).
  • Rubber bands: elastic but NOT Hookean — they return to shape, but their graph is a curve, so force is not directly proportional to extension. The exam loves this distinction. 🧠

Worked example

✏️ Finding k

A spring extends 4 cm (0.04 m) under a 6 N load.
k = F ÷ x = 6 ÷ 0.04 = 150 N/m.
⚠️ Always convert cm → m before dividing!

Springs in series and parallel (extension)

  • Series (end to end): each spring carries the full load → total extension doubles → system is half as stiff (k ÷ 2).
  • Parallel (side by side): load shared between springs → each extends half → total extension halves → system is twice as stiff (k × 2).
  • Intuition: two springs together make it easier to stretch (series) or harder (parallel).

Experiment essentials (1.22 + the 5-mark method question)

  1. Research question: how does the extension of a spring vary with applied force?
  2. IV: force/load (N) · DV: extension (cm/m) · Controls: same spring, same clamp/setup.
  3. Measure the spring's original length with a ruler (read at eye level to avoid parallax error).
  4. Add masses one at a time; record length after each; extension = new length − original length.
  5. Repeat whole run and average; plot force (x) vs extension (y); draw line of best fit; circle anomalies.
  6. Accuracy: ruler at eye level, use a pointer/backing card. Reliability: repeat + average + line of best fit.

Good results table: ① headings = quantity + unit ② units in the heading only ③ repeats taken and averaged ④ same number of decimal places throughout.

Worked examples for the two "hard" formulas

✏️ v² = u² + 2as — stone down a 20 m well

Dropped from rest (u = 0), falls at g = 10 m/s², s = 20 m.
v² = 0 + 2 × 10 × 20 = 400 → v = 20 m/s.

✏️ v² = u² + 2as — plane runway

From rest (u = 0), accelerates at 2.7 m/s² to take-off 57 m/s:
s = 57² ÷ (2 × 2.7) = 3249 ÷ 5.4 ≈ 600 m of runway.

✏️ KE = Work — braking distance

Car 950 kg at 23 m/s, brakes exert 8700 N:
½ × 950 × 23² = 8700 × d → 251 275 = 8700d → d ≈ 29 m.

Common unit conversions

  • km/h → m/s: ÷ 3.6 (50 km/h = 14 m/s, 100 km/h = 28 m/s)
  • cm → m: ÷ 100 (4 cm = 0.04 m)
  • g (grams) → kg: ÷ 1000
  • 1 minute = 60 s · 1 hour = 3600 s

Write the formula first even if you have no idea — physics papers give marks for the formula line. Then substitute, calculate, round to 2 significant figures, and always attach units.

Ready-to-paste mark-scheme phrases ✍️

Vector quantity: "A quantity with both magnitude (size) and direction."
Why terminal velocity: "As the object falls, its speed increases so air resistance increases until it equals the weight. The forces are balanced, the resultant force is zero, so the object falls at constant (terminal) velocity."
Why measured g is lower: "Air resistance acts upwards on the falling object, reducing the resultant force and so the acceleration."
Doubling speed: "Kinetic energy is proportional to v², so doubling speed quadruples the kinetic energy and the braking distance."
Rubber band: "The band is elastic (it returns to its original shape) but it is not Hookean, because its force–extension graph is not a straight line through the origin, so force is not directly proportional to extension."
Circle motion: "It is accelerating because its direction is constantly changing, even though its speed is constant."

The top 10 traps (marks lost every year)

  1. Writing weight in kg instead of N.
  2. Forgetting to convert cm → m (springs) or km/h → m/s (cars).
  3. Using total stretched length instead of extension.
  4. On v–t graphs: mixing up gradient (acceleration) and area (distance).
  5. Half the rectangle: forgetting the ½ in triangle area = ½ × b × h.
  6. Saying terminal velocity = "stops accelerating and stops" — no, it keeps falling at constant speed.
  7. Using F = ma with the total force instead of the unbalanced/resultant force.
  8. Not giving units, or giving "m/s" for acceleration instead of m/s².
  9. Mixing up thinking factors (driver state) with braking factors (road, tyres, mass, speed) in the table question.
  10. Answering an "explain" question with just a description — always include "because… so…".

Graph questions — the checklist examiners use

  • Plotting: sensible scales (use ≥ half the grid), axes labelled with quantity AND unit, points as neat crosses or dots in circles.
  • Line of best fit: straight edge for straight lines; ignore outliers (circle anomalies).
  • Reading: gradient = rise ÷ run using TWO points on the line (not data points); area under v–t = distance (count squares if curved).
  • IV on x-axis, DV on y-axis — always.
