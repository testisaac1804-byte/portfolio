Shrinkage — the number you can't measure, and the fit it doesn't fix

Two HTML calculators and one CLI, built around a single admission: a 40 mm cube cannot resolve the shrinkage it is supposed to measure. The first tool answers “what do I put in my slicer” and answers 100 % more often than any other number. The second answers “how do I make it go in slowly” — which is not a shrinkage problem at all, but a taper.

The constraint that shaped everything

“can you make it that it only calculates the shrinkage percentage” — then, a day later, “dont i need to only change the shrinkage”. The tool was asked twice to be smaller than it was, and the second question is the interesting one: it assumes shrinkage is the knob that fixes a fit. It isn't.

1 · What it is

A shrinkage calculator that accepts several caliper readings per axis, uses the smallest reading on each axis as the true size, averages those minima into one measured size, and then decides whether the difference from nominal is real. If it isn't, the page refuses to hand over a scale factor: the big tile reads 100.00 % and the arithmetic it declined to apply sits underneath it in grey. That refusal is the feature. Every other shrink calculator on the internet multiplies your number by a constant and smiles.

2 · Version ladder — what each pass cut

V1

Four constant-clearance fits

Slip / snug / glue / press, each a fixed gap per side, plus a live SVG cross-section. It answered “what clearance do I use” and produced a number that was wrong for the question actually being asked, because all four presets are the same clearance at the mouth and at the floor. A constant gap can only drop in or jam; neither is “goes in slowly”.

V1.5

Multi-reading inputs, a lock fit, and an insertion ramp

Calipers on a printed face are noisy, so one reading per axis became many, and the datum became the minimum (a tilted caliper only ever reads big). A fifth fit — lock — made the bite a linear ramp over the full depth instead of a constant interference, and a “How it goes in” chart plots gap against insertion distance so the free travel and the bite zone are visible rather than described. What was cut: the illusion that four presets covered the space.

V2

A second page that does one thing

Rather than stripping the fit tool, the shrink maths moved into its own 18 KB page: preset, nominal, three reading boxes, four numbers out, no diagrams. What was cut: pockets, lead-ins, ramps, warnings, and the entire notion that a shrink number implies a fit. One page, one job.

5 · Anatomy — three decisions that carry the tool

The smallest reading, not the average

An outside measurement with a tilted caliper reads too big, never too small — the jaws bridge the part at an angle. So the minimum of several readings is the closest thing to the true flat-to-flat width, while the average is the true width plus a systematic bias. Holes invert: rock and keep the largest. The page implements the outside rule, and it is why a 0.090 mm spread doesn't move the answer as far as it would if the readings were averaged.

A noise floor made of the user's own data

The threshold isn't a magic number: it is half the widest spread across the axes, plus 0.02 mm for caliper and handling slop, floored at 0.05 mm. Type three tight readings and the bar drops; type three sloppy ones and the tool demands more evidence before it will move the slicer. The measurement quality scores itself.

Lock: interference that ramps instead of arriving

The lock fit ramps the gap from +0.16 mm/side at the mouth to −0.04 mm/side at the floor across 20 mm. The cube is guided for 16 mm, then the last 4 mm tightens at 10 µm per mm of push. Same total interference as a press fit, spread over a distance instead of an instant — which is the entire difference between “mallet” and “firm push”.

The taper has to equal the depth

If the taper stops before the floor, the parallel section below it becomes a hard stop and the part jams exactly where the bite ends. The tool warns when the bite zone falls under 1.5 mm (a jolt), when the bite exceeds 0.09 mm/side (walls splay or crack), and when the mouth is opened so wide that the first few millimetres simply fall in loose.

7 · Resolution — why a 40 mm cube can't answer this question

A caliper reads to 0.01 mm. On a 40 mm witness that tick is 0.025 % — half of the effect being measured — and hand-held repeatability is nearer 0.05 mm (0.125 %). No amount of averaging inside one cube escapes that: the instrument, not the sample size, is the limit.

40 mm (this cube)0.025 %0.12 mm — 12 ticks

200 mm bar0.005 %0.60 mm — 60 ticks

This is why the page carries a material preset table (PLA 0.30 %, silk 0.35 %, CF 0.20 %, PETG 0.35 %, ABS 0.70 %) instead of pretending a cube produced a constant. The presets are literature averages: the tool tells you which way your part moved, the table tells you what the polymer usually does.

8 · Why shrinkage doesn't fix a fit

The question that started the second page: “don't I only need to change the shrinkage?” A scale factor multiplies everything, gaps included. Scale both the cube and the pocket to 100.3 % and the 0.16 mm mouth gap becomes 0.1605 mm — a 0.5 µm change in a fit that is measured in tenths of a millimetre. Correction preserves the fit by construction; that is what makes it a correction.

10 · Honest limitations

The tool measures the part, not the polymer. One cube can't separate material shrink from nozzle temperature, cooling, layer time, infill or bed adhesion — those all move the final size, and the calculator has no inputs for any of them. A number produced here is “how much did this part come out small”, which is the question you need answered to fix this part, and a much weaker claim than “PLA shrinks 0.3 %”.
Shrink isn't isotropic, and one scale can't fix that. XY is constrained by layer adhesion and the bed; Z is free to contract, which is why the worked cube reads 39.91 in Y and 40.03 in Z. The page shows all three axes so the disagreement is visible, but the slicer only takes one number — so the answer is a compromise, and the tool says so instead of hiding it behind a mean.
The lock model is geometry, not mechanics. Rigid walls, linear ramp, no viscoelastic creep, no temperature. “0.6°/side self-locks PLA on PLA” is an observation from pushing parts together, not a simulation — treat the bite rate as a starting point, then print one and feel it.
Calipers measure what the jaws touch. The minima rule removes jaw tilt and most of the seam; it cannot remove a raised bead on the face you chose to measure, and it cannot see a bow in the middle of a wall. 400-grit on a flat plate, figure-eights, five light passes — then measure again.

12 · What's next

  • A 100 mm witness bar in the same spool — that is the only change that turns this from “your part is nominal” into a measurement of the material.
  • Temperature logging at print time, so a shrink difference can be attributed to cooling rather than guessed at.
  • A print-and-feel loop for lock: one 20 mm pocket, then 12 mm and 30 mm, and record which one is “smooth” with that spool — the model proposes, the hand decides.

Method. Every figure on this page was re-derived from the artifact or the CLI, not copied from an earlier draft: byte counts and hashes from shasum and the filesystem, shrink and scale from the formulas above in Python, pocket geometry from dt filament fit output pasted verbatim. Layout was verified in a real Chrome at 390/768/1440 px with a headless harness that asserts overflow, tap-target height and console errors, and the maths cross-checked by recomputing each displayed value independently and comparing it to the rendered DOM.

Honesty notes. The material shrink percentages are literature averages and are labelled as such in the tool. The lock-taper behaviour at 0.5–0.6°/side is reported as an observation from printed parts, not as a simulated result. The 40 mm cube used throughout is the user's own and its readings are reproduced exactly, including the single-reading X axis that is too thin a sample to trust on its own.
