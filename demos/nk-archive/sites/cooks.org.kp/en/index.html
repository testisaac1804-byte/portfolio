Corn Noodles Tell Warm Affection for the People

   Noodles are one of the traditional dishes favoured by the Korean people.
   In the past it was considered that the noodles could only be made with buckwheat or potato starch flours. Since they are made with corn easily available in the country, they have gained more popularity, and a corn noodles house was set up on Changgwang Street in downtown Pyongyang.
   However, only few people know the affectionate story associated with the corn noodles.
   One July day in 1987 Chairman Kim Jong Il told the officials to prepare the corn noodles for supper.
   At supper the Chairman entered the room and saw the corn noodles with palatable seasonings and garnishes. Saying how good it would be to prepare corn noodles at this level, he continued to say that ordinary restaurants could not do like this.
   Officials were moved to see the benevolent image of the Chairman who thought people first seeing a bowl of corn noodles.
   The following November the Chairman instructed officials that it was important to make corn noodles tasty in the corn processing. Though our people liked noodles, he added, they could not eat always either buckwheat or starch noodles, so we should decisively improve the quality of corn noodles so that our people would eat noodles to their heart’s content.
   Afterwards, the Chairman paid close attention to improve the quality of corn noodles.
   Even on his birthday in February 1988 the Chairman, who deserved the greatest honour of the people, had the corn noodles and instructed that it was urgent for the present to find the way of improving the quality of the corn noodles.
   On March 16, 1990 he told officials that the researchers of the corn processing research institute under the Academy of Light Industry succeeded in making tasty and nutritious degenerated corn noodles and that was a big success in the technology of corn processing. Then he was very satisfied that the people could make noodles with corn easily available in the country and that it would be a great change in the dietary life of the people.
   Afterwards, he perfected the new processing method of frozen noodles by conducting several tests by himself and gave valuable instructions to generalize the frozen noodles. On February 3, 1991 he said to officials to set up corn noodles houses in various places of Pyongyang.
   Thanks to the sagacious leadership of the Chairman who treasured the national things most, the Korean people could eat noodles as daily meals, though they had regarded it as a special dish for holidays, felt more keenly the excellent qualities of the nation.

Benevolent Father

   On December 7, Juche 95(2006) Chairman Kim Jong Il inspected a unit of the Korean People’s Army.
   After he had a photo session with soldiers, he looked round several places of the unit and dropped in at a mess hall.
   He stopped in front of the board of the plan for “Day for the Soldiers” and asked the unit’s commanding officer standing near him.
   “What do the soldiers like best on the ‘Day for the Soldiers’?”
   “They like everything, but…meat above anything else.”
   “Right! And what meat do they like best?”
   “They like pork best.”
   “You’re right! They like pork best.”
   “Yes, they do.”
   “How much meat do they want?”
   “Well…the more, the better.”
   The Chairman laughed a hearty laugh, acknowledging his answer.
   Then he said to him in whisper in the following vein: Though the soldiers like pork, don’t give them too much meat at one time. And lard should be taken off to be used in making bean-curd dregs.
   At the compartment where the meals were served, the Chairman was very satisfied to know that the portions of the boiled rice for the soldiers increased.
   His beaming image was, indeed, that of a benevolent father who found great pleasure in providing his own children with larger portions of rice.

Standard Regulations Should Be Observed

   One December day in 1984 Chairman Kim Jong Il saw kimchi made at a newly-built kimchi factory.
   Carefully going over kimchi, including its head, colour and even layers, the Chairman said in reproof that kimchi was not prepared properly to be supplied to people because of failure in the observation of standard regulations in maturation process.
   It was known later that the officials of the kimchi factory, in an attempt to hasten the fermentation of kimchi, raised the temperature higher than the normal so that kimchi looked matured but remained raw inside.
   The Chairman instructed that kimchi produced at the kimchi factory should not be inferior in taste to those prepared at home and that the kimchi factory should improve the taste of kimchi.
   He said that kimchi, a favourite dish of the Korean people from long ago, was known as health food good for longevity as it contained vitamins, mineral salts, microelements and digestive enzymes in abundance.
   Then he continued in the following vein: There are many varieties of kimchi typical of regions, including sokbak kimchi (pickled sliced radish and cabbage seasoned with pepper) and sikhye (fermented fish) in Hamgyong Province, whole cabbage kimchi and kkaktugi (cubed radish kimchi) in Phyongan Province, and possam kimchi (wrapped-up kimchi) in Kaesong. Among them the whole cabbage kimchi in Phyongan Province has long been known to be tasty. So the kimchi factory should produce kimchi varieties, with the main emphasis put on the production of whole cabbage kimchi of Phyongan Province, and supply them to people.
   One April day in 1986 the Chairman summoned an official and asked what kind of kimchi was produced at the kimchi factory.
   The official replied that the whole cabbage kimchi was mainly produced and radish kimchi sometimes.
   The Chairman said that in the spring season cubed or sliced radish kimchi seemed preferable to whole cabbage kimchi and our people had prepared a variety of kimchi according to seasons and materials from olden times.
   Noting that kimchi varieties favoured by the people should be prepared and supplied, he continued to say in the following vein: While placing a main stress on making whole cabbage kimchi, it is good to produce sokbak kimchi in the early winter until whole cabbage kimchi is fully matured, and then cubed and sliced radish kimchi, sikhye, tongchimi (pickled whole radish) and other varieties after whole cabbage kimchi. Then our people will be delighted.
   The measure taken by the Chairman to supply a wide variety of kimchi to the people reflected his intention that the officials should make devoted service to the people as well as his patriotic will to develop traditional foods.

Mistake Corrected by the Chairman

   One early April day in 1984 Chairman Kim Jong Il visited a foodstuff factory.
   While looking round several production lines of the factory, he stopped before the rice-cleansing process and asked how the water used in rice washing was treated.
   One official replied that the rice-washing water was first put into the tank in the factory compound and then disposed.
   Then the Chairman said that the rice-washing water would be recycled for making chongju (wine with a low alcohol content) or makkolli (coarse liquor), and explained in detail the method of making makkolli by concentrating the rice-washing water.
   Makkolli, which literally means a coarse liquor, is a traditional drink of Korea with a long history.
   In the past makkolli was made at home by mixing rice-washing water or by-products of rice with malt and fermenting it before filtering.
   It is sweet and sourish containing some 10 per cent of alcohol and thus gives a refreshing taste. It also contains such health-promoting substances as minerals and nutritional and organic substances, hence the custom of relieving fatigue from farming by drinking makkolli during a break or at lunchtime.
   Hearing the instruction of the Chairman, the officials realized even at a cursory calculation that the rice-washing water, which had been previously abandoned, could be recycled to produce more than 200 litres of makkolli a day.
   It was thanks to the Chairman who was always concerned about the improvement of the people’s dietary life and took measures to develop the traditional foods.

How the National Liquor of the DPRK Was Born

   Pyongyangju, national liquor of the DPRK, was born under the concern of Chairman Kim Jong Il.
   The Korean people, who had developed the distillation technology from ancient times, produced Rihwaju (liquor made from pear blossoms), Paekjaju (pine-nut liquor) and other liquors in the Koryo period, and increased the number of liquors to scores of varieties, including Kamhongno, Jungryokko, and Pyokhyangju, in the days of the feudal Joson dynasty.
   At present there are famous liquors in the DPRK, such as Kaesong Koryo Insam Liquor which is named after the first unified state of the Korean nation, Pyongyang Liquor, Paengno Liquor, Taephyong Liquor, and Inphung Liquor.
   Chairman Kim Jong Il, busy as he was with the Songun-based revolutionary leadership, gave instructions on several occasions to develop famous liquor which would be favoured by the people and also represent the state.
   He proposed to build a distillery for producing high-grade alcohol and liquor in August 2006 and chose its site near the Taedonggang Beer Brewery. He took measures to finish the construction in a little over one year and develop the distillery into a base for producing famous liquor.
   The Chairman visited the newly-erected Taedonggang Foodstuff Factory on September 29, 2009.
   Expressing his satisfaction with the fine appearance of the factory building, he went to the sample room to see the liquors on display.
   The factory’s chief engineer showed him the Pyongyang Liquor produced in the test run of the factory. The Chairman said to his accompanying officials to taste it.
   After having tasted the liquor, the Chairman said it tasted better than the previously-produced Ryugyong Soju and Ryugyong Liquor, and continued to say:
   “I’ve got good impression of the Pyongyang Liquor. I have so far seen many liquors boasting of their being renowned, but the Pyongyang Liquor is the best. I will remember the liquor I’ve tasted today.”
   He then named the liquor Pyongyangju.
   He was also very satisfied, when he was told by the chief engineer that the 25-percent Pyongyang Soju, a blended product, tasted good.
   That day he posed for a photograph with factory officials in front of the factory building.
   Pyongyangju, the renowned liquor of Korea, is served in the Okryu Restaurant on the bank of the Taedong River.

Benefits of Flour Foods

   From olden times the Koreans prepared flours by grinding grains with stone grinders, mortars and mills.
   With them, they made noodles, ttok (rice cakes), pancakes and other flour foods.
   Such a long tradition, however, faded away with the passage of time, and some people were inclined to prepare only one or two flour foods and thus led a monotonous dietary life.
   One Sunday in August 1988 Chairman Kim Jong Il met with some officials and talked about the benefits of flour foods in the following vein:
   Foods made of flours are better digested and absorbed than cereal ones and also easy to make. So they are very conducive to promoting people’s health and improving their diet. Corn, for example, when boiled and eaten in uncrushed form, can only be digested by 30-40%, but when degenerated and prepared into rice or noodles, can be absorbed by 90% and more. Flour foods are also good for helping children grow tall and strengthening bones. And they would be conducive to diversifying staple foods and enriching the people’s diet.
   Listening to him, the officials were greatly touched by his devotion to improving the people’s diet.
   That evening, the Chairman invited them to a dinner.
   The officials were amazed to see foods on the table, most of which were prepared with flour.
   Looking at them, the Chairman smiled and said he had the table specially prepared in order to renew their understandings of flour foods.
   It was known afterwards that he gave a task to prepare a dinner featured by foods of wheat and corn flours, a main emphasis put on showing how to make them tasty and diversified.
   With the dinner as a momentum, the officials changed their understanding about flour foods, and afterwards the Korean people devleoped flour foods and made them a favourite diet.

Suitable Vessels

   On April 11, Juche 63 (1974) Chairman Kim Jong Il accompanied President Kim Il Sung on a visit to the Mansugyo Soft Drinks where a variety of foodstuffs produced at the Pyongyang municipal public catering establishments were exhibited.
   Looking round the exhibits, the great leaders were all satisfied.
   Then the Chairman stopped before a tray of Pollack soup and boiled rice with a pitiful expression.
   Officials were wondering what the matter was, looking at the dish so appetizing and smelling good.
   And the Chairman said that the rice bowl was so big that rice looked smaller in quantity.
   The officials took another close look at the bowl and noticed it was too big.
   As sight, as well as taste, is one of major factors in evaluating a dish, and serving a dish in a suitable vessel is a way of establishing a food culture. Therefore, it was not a trivial matter that could be overlooked and it would offend the diners.
   The Chairman, who grasped from the ill-matched bowl the rough workstyle of officials and the actual state of vessel production, instructed that the ceramic factory should produce rice bowls as suited to one consumption of rice and supply them to restaurants and households.
   The officials felt keenly again that they, whose duty is to faithfully serve the people, should be warm-hearted and meticulous.

Noticeboard for Common Food Knowledge

   It happened on November 14 Juche 95 (2006) when Chairman Kim Jong Il visited the Hamju County Farmers’ Holiday Camp after his field guidance to Kumjingang Hungbong Youth Power Station.
   After he looked round inside the dining hall, he had a close look at a noticeboard on the wall and pointed his finger at one spot.
   Accompanying officials got near the board to see that it introduced ill-matched foods. One of them said that bear’s stomach and something are bad for health when taken together. There were also foods that were not widely known and some information was not based on scientific explanation.
   Asking with a big smile that how many of our farmers could taste bear’s stomach, the Chairman instructed that the noticeboard should show how to grow bean sprouts or make kimchi and so on, which would help holiday-makers learn at least one correct common sense during their stay.
   To introduce common sense as suited to the actual conditions and what would be necessary for the living-this is the intention of the Chairman endowed with love for the people.

Dumpling Soup Associated with Benevolent Care

   On the morning of November 8 Juche 70 (1981) Chairman Kim Jong Il had dumpling soup prepared for breakfast and told an official to bring an old comic entertainer.
   To the official looking perplexed, the Chairman said he had long known him, and seen his performance and met him on several occasions. He continued to say that whenever he met him he felt the entertainer’s clear and pure sincerity and, therefore, he trusted and loved him.
   The entertainer came at a run to see the Chairman and made a deep bow to him.
   The Chairman took the entertainer by hand and said that he called him to serve dumpling soup since he liked it.
   The entertainer felt a lump in his throat and bowed his head not to show his tears.
   The Chairman seated the man next to him and had the steaming dumpling soup put before him. Drawing a cruet near him, the Chairman said the dumpling soup would have its genuine taste when taken hot and he would help himself to the soup as there were enough food.
   The entertainer liked dumpling soup very much and preferred it to any other special foods on his birthday or holidays.
   However, he hardly imagined that the Chairman would know his eating habits. Filled with emotion, he told the Chairman he would help himself to the soup and ate it with relish.
   Looking at him with delight, the Chairman recollected the day when he met the latter for the first time and then asked him if he knew that he had narrowly escaped from being expelled from the circus.
   Several years ago some officials found faults with his personal records and tried to transfer him to another post.
   Being informed of it, the Chairman rejected the suggestion.
   The entertainer, who was totally unaware of that, could not find words to express his thanks but burst out sobbing.
   At the moment he thought of Charlie Chaplin, once the greatest comic genius in the 20th century.
   Chaplin, born of a poor entertainer’s family, rose to “comic star of the century” through painstaking endeavours, but he had no embrace to defend him.
   His comedies reflected his sympathy with proletarians and scathing lampoons of exploiter classes, thus evoking resentment of the ruling class. He was subjected to all sorts of persecution and died in an alien land.
   On the contrary, the entertainer enjoyed high reputation for scores of years in the embrace of the great leaders.
   A bowl of dumpling soup the Chairman prepared for him was an expression of the affection of the benevolent leader who brought an ordinary entertainer up as the one beloved by the people and took meticulous care of his development.
   

Memory of Potato Taffy

   Chairman Kim Jong Il visited on February 23 Juche 91 (2002) the Taehongdan Potato Processing Factory which underwent reconstruction on an expansion basis.
   Looking round the factory’s production lines of dehydrated starch noodles, taffy, liquor and so on, the Chairman acquainted himself with the processing equipment and products and expressed his satisfaction.
   At the taffy production workshop he saw the jet liquefier in operation and instructed that such production line should be generalized in foodstuff factories across the country.
   Looking at the snow-white potato taffy being ejected from the machine, he said that when he visited Pochonbo on a study tour in the postwar days he ate potato taffy and that though it was blackish it tasted good.
   Several days later the Chairman met the senior officials of the Party Central Committee and recalled the past days again, saying in the following vein:
   When I saw the potato taffy at that factory I remembered that I ate potato taffy in Pochonbo in 1956 on my study tour of revolutionary battlesites in Mt Paektu. The granny of the house offered us potato taffy in dark colour, and it was delicious. The taffy produced at the Taehongdan Potato Processing Factory was white.
   The officials were moved by his words.
   It was only a stick of potato taffy that people could serve the Chairman who visited the area of Mt Paektu in the difficult days of the postwar rehabilitation.
   But the Chairman enjoyed the taffy stick and made a firm determination to make the people there well off. And to realize the determination of that day he had a modern potato processing factory with taffy production line erected in the area when a radical change was being effected in potato farming.
   As he was glad to have his people-oriented intention realized, he remembered with deep emotion the taffy offered by a granny in Ryanggang Province nearly 50 years ago.
   He continued to say to the officials.
   “We should produce potato taffy in large amounts and supply to people…. We should provide children with taffy even though we fail to give them candy.”
   The officials were again deeply moved by his warm affection bestowed on the people.

Excellent Qualities of Korean Dishes

  • Taste and Fragrance

    Taste and Fragrance

       Traditional dishes of Korea have light, fresh and pleasant tastes and fragrant and faint smells.
       They are neither too greasy, sweet, salty or saltless, nor strong-scented.
       What makes Korean dishes palatable is that they are made to sustain unique tastes of raw foods, such as cereals, meat, fish, vegetables, edible herbs, fruits and so on, and harmonize with each other.
       Either processed or stored materials can be used in cooking, but in most cases fresh ones are used.
    Soy sauce and bean paste are more preferable to salt, and various seasonings and spices including chilli, honey, sesame, pepper, mustard, leek, garlic, and ginger, are used appropriately.
       Koreans have long created and developed various culinary methods, and actively used chilli, garlic and sesame oil, making Korean dishes distinctive from foreign cuisines.
       Generally speaking, Korean dishes that prioritize natural, fresh taste and produce at the same time excellent combination of flavours-simple, sweet, delicious, refreshing and pleasant-are conducive to stimulating appetite and nutritive intake and promoting health.

  • Beautiful Colours

    Beautiful Colours

       Traditional Korean dishes have bright and mellow colours and nice appearances.
       Colours and appearances of dishes are important factors in increasing appetite.
       In preparing dishes Koreans avoided too high heat or too much oil, so as to retain inherent colours of raw materials.
       They widely used such edible plants as Schizandra chinensis, pine-tree bark, jujube, chilli, garnedia seed, pine pollen, wormwood, angelica leaf, and green perilla to give various colours to dishes.
       Gingko, pine nut, walnut, jujube, chestnut, chilli, leek, egg, mushroom and others were also applied to improve visual effects.

  • Diversity

    Diversity

       Korean dishes amount to several thousand in kind, and they are diverse in shape.
       Staple and nonstaple foods, the sweetened, drinks and other Korean foods are prepared with a wide variety of agricultural and marine products and by means of different culinary methods.
       Rice, a major staple diet of the Korean people, and kimchi, their typical relish, have a wide variety.
       For example, Koreans used pumpkin and its leaves and stalks in preparing boiled rice, rice cake, pancake, congee, soup, kimchi, sautéed, broiled and seasoned dishes, and others.

  • Health Foods

    Health Foods

       Korean dishes are very good for promoting health and longevity.
       Main cooking materials contain a balanced proportion of nutrients essential for human body.
       A long intake of them hardly has negative effects but is pharmacologically effective.
       Glutinous rice, radish, bok choy, sesame, jujube, ginger, leek, garlic, gingko, pine-nut, chestnut, walnut and other grains, vegetables, fish, meat, and wild fruits were principal stuffs for staple and side dishes of the Korean people as well as for medical treatment. Not only them but also anise, aralia shoots, broad bellflower, Codonopsis lanceolata, wormwood, seaweed, kelp and laver with medicinal qualities were much used in cooking.
       For this reason, Korean dishes have long been famous for promoting health, preventing diseases and helping medical treatment.
       Among such dishes are yakpap and ogokpap, boiled rices with pharmacological effects; steamed chicken with insam and steamed chicken and carp with invigorating and hematic effects; and various teas of Schizandra chinensis, pear, dried persimmon and ginger efficacious for strengthening heart, stomach and other organs.
       And condimentary stuffs were known to have high medicinal properties.
       Pharmacological effects of the steamed foods were enhanced by various vegetable foodstuffs, such as mushroom, chestnut, gingko, radish and kelp. After eating rice wrapped in lettuce leaves, people would drink warm cinnamon tea so as not to suffer indigestion.
       Medicinal herbs were usually used in preparing congees, alcoholic beverages, sweets and even foods for famine relief.
       In the course of age-old endeavours to transform the nature, the Korean people pooled their wisdom and efforts to actively introduce useful things to their life, dietary life in particular, and turn them into delicious foods favourable for physiology and national tastes by means of various culinary methods.
       That is why the Korean dishes are numerous in kind, palatable, eye-catching in colour and appearance, and good for health.

   Since the dawn of human civilization the Korean nation has lived on the same territory as a homogenous nation, and they have created and developed a peculiar dietary culture.
   Korean dishes that are prepared with diverse materials rich in mountains, fields, rivers and seas constitute one of valuable cultural heritage.
   Kimchi, sinsollo, Pyongyang cold noodles, pulgogi, mung bean pancake and other traditional dishes conform to physical constitutions and tastes of the Korean people, so they are popular not only at home but also abroad.
   Numbering several thousand items in kind, they are palatable, aromatic, and beautiful in colour and appearance. Methods of preparing them are also diverse and yet unique. Scores of dishes are prepared with one material.
   Many experts say that Korean dishes fully reflect aspiration and wish of the people for long life in good health, and that they are the world models in terms of nutritive and hygienic values.
   Korean dishes whose excellence is proved scientifically attract a growing number of people and show a steady improvement.

   Jongwoldaeborum, the 15th day of the first lunar month, is one of folk holidays celebrated by the Korean people from ancient times.
   Festive dishes for the day include ogokpap, yakpap, pokssam, noodles, dried herb dishes, and ear-quickening liquor.
   Ogokpap is the boiled rice mixed with four other cereals, typically foxtail millet, sorghum, bean and adzuki bean.
   Yakpap, medicinal rice, is prepared with glutinous rice and honey as major ingredients, and also jujube, dried persimmon, chestnut, pine nuts, sesame oil and others.
   Nine kinds of cooked dried herbs are good for not getting ill from the summer heat.
   Koreans of old times favoured these dishes, because such balanced diet would help the intake of various nutrients for health promotion.
   They ate noodles at lunch the day before, wishing that they would live as long as the noodle strips.

   The hottest period of summer is called sambok in Korean.
   From olden times people went to summer resorts and prepared seasonable dishes.
   As a proverb says, Like cures like, they ate hot tangogi soup, samgyethang, spicy beef soup, aduzuki bean congee, boiled barley rice and leek soup.
   Tangogi soup is prepared with slices of well-cooked tangogi garnished with minced skin, oil, seasoning herbs, garlic, and powders of red and black peppers and served in boiling hot tangogi broth.
   Samgyethang is a dish of jugged young chicken with insam, jujube, glutinous rice and other ingredients.

Dietary Custom

  • Major staple and nonstaple foods

    Major staple and nonstaple foods

       The Korean people ate boiled cereals as their staple food since they put main stress on crop production.
       In the early days of their civilizations humans lived on various foods according to their natural surroundings and productivity.
       Those in the mountainous areas hunted animals for food, while those along the rivers and seas eating shellfish and fish for staple foods.
       With the growth of crop cultivation in the post-Neolithic era, cereals took major places in diet in the form of boiled cereals, congees and cereal cakes.
       Though foods made of cereals have become staple foods, they vary according to nations.
       From olden times bread was a major food in European countries, but the Koreans had boiled cereals as a staple food in everyday diet and rice cake as a special food.
       Such a dietary custom had been carried forward from ancient times and middle ages to contemporary days, becoming a tradition of the Korean nation.
       Among a wide variety of nonstaple foods, major ones are soups, soybean paste and sauce, and kimchi.
       Korean people never excluded soups, soybean paste and sauce, and kimchi for their everyday meals, though other kinds of nonstaple foods were occasionally served.
       Traditionally they highly prized bean paste soups, and boiling stew in bean paste served in earthen vessel was a sight unique to Korean meals.
       Kimchi is another indispensable food.
       In the feudal Joson dynasty holiday feasts included a variety of meat foods, rice cakes and other special dishes, and refreshing kimchi and bean paste soups were a must on the table.
       For this reason, kimchi, soybean paste and sauce, and soups became widely known even to foreign countries as specialities of Korea.

    The custom of managing dietary life assiduously and frugally

       The Korean people traditionally managed their dietary life assiduously and frugally.
       It is seen in the old documents that the people from the Koguryo period economized on food and held wedding and other ceremonies in a simple way.
       It has also been regarded as one of good traits for women to keep their kitchens clean and utensils unstained, glossy and long-lasting.
       An old record shows that in Ancient Joson, the first state of Korea, even poor peasants got necessary bowls for food, which tells the origin of the traditional custom of the Korean people to manage their life clean and assiduously.
       Traditionally the Korean women wore kerchiefs and white aprons when cooking in the kitchen.
       It is proved by the mural painting of a woman in attendance found in the Anak Tomb No. 2 of Koguryo and that of a woman in dress and an apron seen in the Kamsin Tomb.
       Wearing a kerchief and an apron while cooking food is good for preventing hair falling, ensuring hygienic safety and convenient activities and also good in appearance.
       The fact that the Koreans wore kerchiefs and aprons from several thousand years ago eloquently proves the high cultural level of the Korean nation.

  • Habit of eating three meals a day

    Habit of eating three meals a day

       In the earliest stage of human history people wandered from place to place in groups and had irregular meals when they found food.
       But, after the Neolithic Period, when a productive life, involving sedentary agriculture and domesticated animal raising, became predominant with the clan as the unit, they could store up a certain amount of the produce.
       So they got rid of the habit of wandering in search of food, and settled down in a certain place and led a regular life. They slept at home, got up in the morning and had breakfast, went to work, and returned home when it got dark to have supper.
       And in the course of this, they acquired a habit of eating meals at proper intervals, for instance, in the morning and in the evening, or in the morning, in the middle of the day and in the evening.
       Such an eating habit was good for health promotion of the people, as it alleviated the burden of the stomach, and was suitable for labour and daily life.
       The custom of having three meals a day was first recorded in the days of the Three Kingdoms.
       History of the Three Kingdoms tells that a king of Silla had meals two or three times a day and a huge amount of rice, liquor and meat were consumed.
       There are several records of the eating habit in the documents of Koryo and feudal Joson dynasties.
       According to them, during the days of Koryo there lived a man named Hwang Su in the Walled City of Pyongyang, and his family ate from the same pot. He had delicious dishes prepared and served his parents three times a day, and then ate with all other family members. And during the days of feudal Joson dynasty a government official had three meals every day.
       Of the three meals breakfast and supper were considered important, so they included newly prepared boiled cereals, soups and several side dishes. And lunch was comparably light, with either the dishes spared at breakfast or noodles.

  • Traditional Drinks and Their Excellence

    Traditional Drinks and Their Excellence

       Traditional drinks of Korea that reflect the wisdom and talent of the Korean people have been created and developed through a long period of time.
       They boast excellent tastes, medicinal properties, processing methods, varieties and usage.
       They retain characteristics of ingredients well to give unique flavour.
       For example, honeyed juices that are made with fresh fruits taste refreshing, light and fragrant.
       Traditional drinks are also free from astringent, bitter and other impure tastes, so they have exhilarating aftertastes.
       That’s why they are very popular among not only Koreans but also foreigners.
       And their high medicinal values are very beneficial to health promotion and longevity.
       Generally, they are made of ingredients with medicinal properties, including insam, barley, Schizandra chinensis, fruit of Crataegus pinnatifida and matrimony vine.
       Insam tea of Korea is very famous for its remarkable functions of relieving fatigue, lowering blood pressure, acting against cancer, and preventing and treating gastric ulcer. It is effective in promoting mental functions and enhancing resistance to disease.
       Traditional drinks of Korea are widely known across the world as excellent tonics.
       They are also made with scientific accuracy.
       The Korean people applied scientific principles to the manufacturing of traditional drinks from long ago, and exerted a considerable influence on the development of drinks of the neighbouring countries.
       Typical traditional drinks include sujonggwa (honeyed juice with dried persimmon, pine nuts and cinnamon), sikhye (sweet rice drink), hyangsolgo (boiled pear preserved in honey), hwachae (honeyed juice with fruit), and kamju (sweet drink prepared with rice and malt).

    Custom of having meals on a table in an underfloor-heated room

       It is a traditional dietary custom of the Korean people to set foods on a table and enjoy meals in a warm underfloor-heated room with all family members.
       From ancient times the Korean people lived in wooden houses with beams built on a floor-heating device, which are similar to present rural houses.
       Previous stoves warmed half-underground shacks to a certain degree, but they were not enough for warm houses built on the gound.
       So the Korean ancestors built kitchen ranges inside houses and introduced floor-heating system to use heat without waste in warming rooms and living in comfort.
       In Koryo and feudal Joson dynasties underfloor-heating systems were disseminated throughout the country, and the custom of having meals on a table in a warm room was passed on through generations.

History & Episodes

  • Pancake

    Pancake

       In olden times people ate stuffed pancakes, and since the 18th-19th century they made pancakes without stuffing. Pancakes are called jijim, or jonbyong, or puchigi in Korean.
       In some regions, such as Hwanghae Province, pancakes were prepared by filling crushed adzuki bean or mung bean.
       Pancakes are made with various cereals, such as mung bean, potato, corn, wheat, sorghum, glutinous rice and adzuki bean. Either new crops of the year are watered and milled or powdered cereals are kneaded to fry in oil.
       It is easy to make pancakes, so they are a common dish for every family.

  • Porridge

    Porridge

       Porridge was an alternative staple food for the Korean people.
       They ate porridge since the primitive times when they began to grow cereals, that is, even before they ate boiled cereals.
       Historical records and folk materials show that there were a wide variety of porridge which were prepared in various ways and had great pharmacological effects.
       During the feudal Joson dynasty many families prepared porridge for breakfast.
       And old people ate porridge before breakfast, and weak or ill people ate porridge for health recuperation.
       It was also a folk custom to send porridge prepared with rice and adzuki to a mourner’s house.
       Porridge is made with cereals, sometimes added with various ingredients.

  • Jelly

    Jelly

       Jellies are prepared by boiling starches obtained from ground buckwheat, mung bean, corn or acorn and cooling them.
       In old historical documents jelly, or muk in Korean, was written as pho in Chinese character.
       Mung bean jelly was regarded as the best of the jellies, and the jelly made of yellow mung bean was called hwangpho (yellow-coloured jelly) and that of green mung bean chongpho (greenish jelly).
       Korean people had long eaten mukchae, a salad made with jelly and other ingredients.
       Jinchanuigwe, a book compiled in 1848, tells that jelly salad made of green mung bean includes as secondary ingredients pork, parsley, laver, egg and chili powder. Another historical book, Tongguksesigi, refers to mung bean jelly salad as thangphyongchae, which also includes pork, parsley, laver and vinegared soy sauce.
       In the past ordinary families usually ate jellies made of acorn, buckwheat and corn for lunch or supper, but they prepared mung bean jelly as a special dish for holiday meals or when they entertained guests.
       Noodles made of green corn jelly served in refreshing kimchi juice or sesame soup was regarded as a special dish in the Phyongan provincial area in fresh corn season.

  • Cereal Cakes

    Cereal Cakes

       Cereal cakes are made in the way of steaming cereals or cereal powder, or pounding or moulding the boiled cereals.
       They are one of favourite staple foods of the Korean people.
       The historical fact that the Korean nation made cereal cakes already in the Bronze Age, some 4 000 years ago, when they made earthenware steamers can be proven by a mural painting found in the tomb of King Kogukwon of Koguryo Kingdom.
       And the Ballad of Mill composed by Paekkyol, a musician in the days of the Three Kingdoms, shows that there was a custom of making cereal cakes as festive dishes for the New Year.
       Cereal cakes are prepared as special dishes for the birthday party, wedding ceremony and holidays.
       There are anecdotes, stories and proverbs related to cereal cakes as many as those related to boiled cereals.

  • Boiled cereals

    Boiled cereals

       The Korean people’s eating of boiled cereals as a staple food was formed in primitive times.
       Korean ancestors cultivated barnyard millet, foxtail millet, millet, sorghum, soybean, azuki bean and rice already in primitive and ancient times.
       The word pap, boiled cereals in English, appeared in written documents for the first time in the early middle ages, the period of Three Kingdoms.
       Samguksagi and Samgukyusa, old chronicles and history book of the Three Kingdoms in Korea, recorded pap and chalpap (boiled glutinous rice).
       Pap was called pakko during the Koryo dynasty.
       Pap is cooked in various ways, with one cereal, with two or three cereals, and with other supplementary materials.
       Many proverbs were created in relation with pap.
       An old Korean proverb says, The spectacles of famous Mt Kumgang cannot interest the hungered, meaning nothing can work on an empty stomach.
       Such proverbs as A grain of rice kills ten ghosts, Boiled rice is medicine, and Good health is beneath the rice bowl, give medical advices that it is important to have regular meals in order to be in good health as well as restore health.
       Besides, there are many other proverbs related with boiled cereals.
