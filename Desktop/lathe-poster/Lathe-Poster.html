1 ANATOMY OF AN ENGINE LATHE (CENTRE LATHE)

A lathe holds the workpiece and spins it about its own axis while a single-point cutting tool is pressed against it. Because the work rotates, every feature is symmetrical about the axis — cylinders, cones, threads, spheres, bores. It is the “mother of machine tools”: it can machine the parts to build every other machine tool, including itself.

Workpiece ROTATES about its axis (orange arrow); the tool is fed along the bed (longitudinal) or across it (facing) to remove material. The leadscrew synchronises carriage travel with spindle rotation so screw threads cut perfectly.

3 LATHE OPERATIONS — WHAT YOU CAN CUT

Facing
Square the end of the work; tool feeds across the end face. First operation after chucking.
Straight turning
Reduce diameter along the length — roughing (deep cuts) then finishing (light cuts, fine feed, accurate size).
Taper turning
Cones by swivelled compound, offset tailstock, taper attachment or CNC — e.g. Morse tapers 1.5°–3°.
Grooving / parting
Narrow tool cuts a groove (O-ring seats) or parts off the piece; thin tools — slow speed, firm workholding.
Knurling
Hardened wheels press a diamond/straight grip pattern into handles — light pressure, slow speed, oil.
Thread cutting
Single-point tool + leadscrew sync cuts 60° V-threads; dies cut external, taps cut internal threads.
Drilling
Tailstock quill feeds the drill into rotating work — centre-drill first so it can’t wander.
Boring
Single-point boring bar enlarges/straightens holes; corrects holes drilled off-centre.
Reaming
Finish a drilled hole to exact size and smoothness — removes only ~0.1–0.3 mm.
Eccentric turning
Work between two offset centres makes crank pins and cam lobes.
Filing / polishing
Slow speed, draw-file or emery cloth to smooth and finish the surface.
Hard turning
Turn hardened steel (45–65 HRC) with CBN inserts — replaces grinding on many jobs.
Also possible: chamfering & deburring, spherical turning (form tool / CNC arc), tapping (internal threads via tailstock), and form-tool profile cutting.

4 WORKHOLDING, MEASURING & TOOLING

Workholding

  • 3-jaw scroll chuck — self-centring, fastest; runout ~0.05–0.1 mm.
  • 4-jaw independent — each jaw dialed in; squares & irregular; true to 0.01 mm.
  • Collet / ER chuck — precise ±0.005 mm; thin & bar stock, no jaw marks.
  • Between centres — dead/live centre + dog; most accurate for long shafts.
  • Faceplate — straps & bolts hold irregular castings.
  • Steady / follower rests — support long slender work; stop chatter.
  • Mandrel — pressed into the bore so the outside turns true to it.

Measuring

  • Vernier caliper 0.02 mm · micrometer 0.01 mm · dial indicator 0.01 mm · DRO 0.005 mm.
  • Check diameter at 3 positions, runout & taper — spindle stopped.

Tool materials

  • HSS — tough, cheap, grindable; ~600 °C max; the manual-lathe default. Cobalt for tough alloys.
  • Carbide — brazed or indexable inserts; 3–4× HSS speeds; rigid machines only.
  • Coated carbide (TiN/TiAlN) — less friction, longer life.
  • Ceramic — cast iron & hardened steel at very high speeds.
  • CBN — hard turning 45–65 HRC. PCD — aluminium & composites.

5 SPEEDS, FEEDS & DEPTH OF CUT

Cutting speed (CS) is the workpiece surface speed in m/min — set by material, tool and operation, not the dial:

RPM = (CS × 1000) ÷ (π × D)D = diameter in mm · π ≈ 3.1416 · CS = π × D × RPM ÷ 1000 · Feed rate (mm/min) = feed (mm/rev) × RPM
Example: Ø40 mm mild steel, HSS, CS ≈ 35 m/min → RPM = 35 000 ÷ 125.7 = ≈ 278 RPM; carbide (CS 140) → ≈ 1114 RPM. Smaller Ø = higher RPM; bigger Ø = lower.

Rules of thumb

  • Depth of cut: roughing 1–4 mm (HSS) / 2–8 mm (carbide); finishing 0.1–0.5 mm. Chatter = too fast/deep, dull tool, too much overhang — slow down.
  • Threading: several light passes, finish with a zero-cut spring pass. Coolant: soluble oil (steel), paraffin (aluminium), dry (cast iron).

6 TYPES OF LATHES

  • Engine / centre lathe — classic general-purpose manual lathe; belts & gears; turning, facing, threading.
  • Bench / mini lathe — small; models, jewellery, electronics, hobby work.
  • Toolroom lathe — highest manual precision; fine leadscrew, close tolerances; tool & die work.
  • Gap-bed lathe — removable bed section swings very large diameters past the carriage.
  • Turret / capstan lathe — multi-tool turret for repeat production batches.
  • CNC turning centre — computer control; canned threading, live tooling, C-axis milling, bar feeders.
  • Swiss-type lathe — sliding headstock + guide bushing; ultra-small precise parts (watch screws, medical).
  • Vertical lathe (VTL) — vertical spindle for heavy, large-diameter discs & flywheels.
  • Wood lathe — hand-held gouges & chisels on a tool rest; work turns, tool guided by hand.
  • Metal-spinning lathe — presses sheet metal discs over a rotating former.
Lathe vs mill: a lathe rotates the work past a fixed tool (circles about the axis: shafts, threads, bores); a mill rotates the tool and moves the work (flats, slots, 3D shapes). Turning centres add milling via live tooling; turn-mill centres do both in one setup.

7 SAFETY & DAILY CARE

Before you touch the spindle

  • No gloves, long sleeves, loose hair or jewellery near a rotating chuck — they wrap and pull you in.
  • Remove the chuck key before starting — a thrown key is a lethal missile.
  • Stop the spindle before measuring, adjusting, cleaning or changing tools.
  • Safety glasses always; clear swarf with a brush/hook, never bare hands.
  • Secure work firmly; right speed; listen for chatter; never grab the chuck to stop it — use the brake. Guards closed, ventilated; know the E-stop.

Daily care

  • Brush chips after every job; wipe the ways — grit grinds the slides.
  • Oil ways, leadscrew & gears daily; a thin film stops rust.
  • Check & adjust gibs (no backlash) and belt tension; clean chuck jaws, re-check runout.
  • Align tailstock to headstock (test between centres); check tool tip height; keep electrical box dry.

8 WHY IT MATTERS

Every engine, gearbox, axle, bolt, bearing and screw on Earth is shaped by a lathe or by machines the lathe built. Without Maudslay’s screw-cutting lathe (1797) there would be no standardised threads, no interchangeable parts, no mass production. Today’s CNC turning centres hold ±0.001 mm and run unattended overnight — yet the manual engine lathe is still the first machine every machinist learns on, because it shows directly how metal behaves under a cutting edge.

Facts

  • Wood lathes hold the tool by hand; metal lathes clamp it rigidly — same motion, different rules.
  • Watch screws from Swiss lathes are thinner than a human hair; the largest lathes turn ship propeller shafts many metres long.
  • A lathe is the only machine tool that can build a copy of itself.
